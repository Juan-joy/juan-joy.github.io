---
title: "Should Singapore Still Invest in Energy and Chemicals If It Wants to Be Net Zero by 2050?"
date: 2026-04-10
---
Every time I tell someone I am a chemical engineer who wants to work in Singapore's energy and chemicals sector, I get some version of the same question. Isn't that industry dying? Doesn't Singapore want to be net zero by 2050? Aren't you choosing a career in something the country is actively trying to shrink?
It's a fair question. I just don't think it has the answer most people assume. So here's my take on it.
## The Singapore contradiction (I squinted at it)

Here are two facts that are both true.
&#x20;
Singapore has committed to net zero emissions by 2050, with an interim target of cutting emissions by 45-50% by 2035. It runs the strongest carbon tax in Southeast Asia. It has embedded decarbonisation targets into the mandates of agencies that would, in any other country, be purely industrial - EDB, JTC, MTI.
Singapore is also home to Jurong Island, a 3,000-hectare industrial complex hosting over 100 energy and chemicals companies, producing about a quarter of national manufacturing output, and accounting for more than half of the country's total emissions. In October 2025, the government celebrated Jurong Island's 25th anniversary by announcing that it would build nine hydrogen ready power plants on the island by 2030, pilot ammonia bunkering, scale sustainable aviation fuel production, and attract over 30 new specialty chemicals projects.
&#x20;
On first read, those two things don't fit together. A country serious about net zero should be winding down its largest emitting industry, not investing more in it.
But if you live in Singapore and you've thought about energy for more than five minutes, you know why the second fact exists despite the first, and actually because of it.

## Singapore has nowhere to hide

Most countries decarbonising heavy industry have options. Germany can build onshore wind. Australia can build utility-scale solar across thousands of square kilometres of nothing. France can lean on nuclear. Norway can electrify almost anything because its grid is already 98% hydro.
&#x20;
Singapore has none of that. No wind to speak of. No space for utility scale solar beyond rooftops and the occasional floating array. No geothermal, no hydro, no uranium mines, no long transmission corridor to anywhere. The country is what the government politely calls "alternative energy disadvantaged", which is a way of saying that if energy transition depended purely on domestic renewable resources, Singapore would lose.
This changes the shape of the problem. For Singapore, decarbonisation cannot mean "stop making things." It has to mean "make things differently, using imports, using better processes, and using the sites that already exist." The alternative isn't a clean Singapore. The alternative is a Singapore that has outsourced its emissions to Indonesia or Vietnam while losing the jobs, the tax base, and the technical workforce that used to sit on Jurong Island.
So the question isn't whether to invest in energy and chemicals. It's what to invest in.

## What the money is actually going toward

If you read the Sustainable Jurong Island plan and the 2025 EDB announcements, the investment pattern is pretty consistent.
&#x20;
The first bucket is low-carbon power generation. Nine hydrogen-ready power plants by 2030, collectively capable of contributing more than 3.7 GW, set against a forecast peak demand of 10-12 GW. An ammonia pathfinder project for both power and marine bunkering. A 326 MWh battery energy storage system already running, one of the largest in Southeast Asia. These are not sunset assets. They are the base layer of a grid that has to stay reliable while the fuel mix shifts.
&#x20;
The second bucket is specialty chemicals and sustainable materials. Thirty-plus new projects since 2021 from companies like Arkema, Cariflex, and Kuraray. Specialty chemicals are expected to grow at 4–5% annually through 2030, compared with 2.5–3.5% for chemicals overall. These are higher-value, lower-volume products, things like bio-based polyamides for medical devices, or EVOH copolymers for food packaging, where the margins come from process expertise, not throughput. It plays to Singapore's strengths and away from its weaknesses.
&#x20;
The third bucket is circular and low-carbon technology testbeds. A $62.5 million A*STAR facility for piloting low-carbon industrial tech. Mura Technology's advanced plastic recycling facility within the PCS complex. Shell's pyrolysis oil upgrades. Croda's wastewater treatment pilot that cuts emissions by 25%. The common thread is that Jurong Island is becoming a place where emerging technologies get tried at industrial scale, with shared utilities and a concentrated customer base to de-risk the economics.
&#x20;
The fourth, which doesn't always get discussed in the same breath but is structurally linked, is carbon pricing and a carbon services hub. Singapore's carbon tax is already the strongest in the region and is scheduled to rise. The government has positioned the country as a regional carbon services and trading centre. The point here is that if you're going to keep a heavy industry sector, you need a pricing mechanism that forces it to decarbonise from the inside, plus the financial infrastructure to service the whole region's transition.
&#x20;
Taken together, none of this looks like doubling down on fossil chemistry. It looks like using an existing industrial footprint as the place where the transition actually gets built.

## The harder question: is this actually going to work?
Honestly, I don't know. And anyone who tells you they do is selling something.
&#x20;
So when someone asks me whether Singapore should still invest in energy and chemicals if it wants to be net zero by 2050, my honest answer is that the two things aren't in tension. Getting to net zero without investing in this sector is the scenario that doesn't add up.
